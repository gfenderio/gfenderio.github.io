Marketplace & Online Store Tools Used daily

Shopee, Tokopedia and TikTok Shop, run from one place

Selling on three marketplaces means three sets of prices, promos and reports that rarely agree. I built the tools the e-commerce team uses to keep them straight. Store prices and discount quotas are set from one screen, and each marketplace’s discount list is imported as a file instead of typed in. The monthly report breaks net revenue down by channel and splits cancellations by cause. On the payment side, every order shows its voucher and net price, and bulk checkouts no longer drift by a rounding error when the total is split. There is also a Chrome extension that puts Kyou’s own controls straight into the Shopee seller page.

  • Go
  • PHP
  • Laravel
  • MySQL
  • Chrome Extension
One Price, Everywhere Used daily

The marketplace price model in the data warehouse

Kyou’s numbers used to come straight from raw logs, and every screen worked them out a little differently. The team built a data warehouse that rebuilds clean, tested tables on a schedule. My part in it is the table that settles what each item sells for on the marketplaces. Then I moved the tools onto the data warehouse, so they stop doing their own sums: the store price screens, the warehouse app, the daily boards and the stock history. Where the history is incomplete, the screen now says so instead of guessing.

  • SQL
  • MySQL
  • Go
  • Data warehouse
The Company’s Working Platform Used daily

Second-largest contributor to the codebase

Kyou used to run on spreadsheets and a PHP admin panel written years ago. I’m moving it into one platform, a process at a time: stock, price changes, stock rotation, courier rates, payment confirmations. I build each one end to end, from the screen to the API to the tables. The old system stays live while this happens, so I maintain it too and wire the two together. Payment confirmation, for one, can be pointed at the old system or the new one with a setting, without a redeploy. I also built the sales dashboard the managers read every morning.

The Warehouse App Used daily

About 70% of the app is mine, now on version 2.45

An Android app the warehouse staff carry all day: counting stock, picking and packing orders, checking in deliveries, and ringing up sales at events. I built barcode scanning through the phone camera, a stock count that starts at the shelf and asks for a photo as proof, and a check-in several people can work on at once without overwriting each other. Online orders show when they were requested and turn red once they are a day late. One screen keeps working with no signal, because parts of the warehouse have none. Cost prices are left out on purpose: floor staff see what they need and nothing more.

  • Kotlin
  • Jetpack Compose
  • Room
  • Hilt
  • Android
The Goods-In Label Printer Used daily

25 releases, and it updates itself

When a delivery arrives, some of it belongs on the shop shelves and some of it has already been bought by a customer. Both turn up in the same box, and mixing them up means somebody loses the thing they paid for. This desktop app reads what came in and prints a small sticker for each item. Anything already sold gets its own mark and the buyer’s reference, so it can’t end up on a shelf by mistake. It only ever reads. It cannot change stock or orders, by design.

  • Electron
  • React 19
  • TypeScript
  • Vite
The Fulfilment Bot Used daily

Running every day since May

A bot that connects the warehouse with the people running it. Proof photos follow every order through picking, packing and handover, and file themselves where they belong. Split shipments are worked out and sent to print without anyone chasing the paperwork. An order stuck halfway through picking is raised with whoever can unstick it, the moment it stalls. The recurring jobs run on schedule, without anyone having to remember them.

  • Node.js
  • TypeScript
  • Docker

About.

I build software people rely on to get through their working day. At Kyou that covers the whole path of an order: the marketplace price and promo tools, payments and reports, the Android app on the warehouse floor, the label printer for goods coming in, and the bot that ties them together. The pattern is nearly always the same. Find the job someone still does by hand, then replace it with something that holds up on a busy day.

Before any of this I spent years on shop floors, running stores and departments, chasing sales targets, keeping stock and prices straight. That wasn’t a detour. It’s the reason the software works. I already knew where a business quietly loses hours, which numbers people have stopped trusting, and why a screen that is technically correct can still be useless at seven in the morning with a queue building.

I’m an Information Systems student, and I learn by building things that get used. If you want someone who understands the shop floor as well as the code, let’s talk.
